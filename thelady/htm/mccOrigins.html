500–300 BCE

The declaration of the "Most High" provided a definitive theological exit from the dominant religious systems of the ancient world. It required Abraham to abandon the complex, manipulative magic of the Mesopotamian pantheon in favor of a relationship—grounded in a binding covenant—with the singular, supreme Source of reality. Furthermore, the enthroned, polytheistic figures within that ancient system are exposed as responsible for the collapse of the Original Design and the systematic degradation of humanity. Consequently, when Psalm 110 later invokes the "order of Melchizedek," it points directly back to this primordial monotheism—a pure faith antedating the formation of nations—to define the ultimate identity of the King of Jerusalem.

To justify King David claiming to be the legitimate heir to the royal priesthood and power of Melchizedek, an ancient Near Eastern ruler could invoke a powerful, multi-layered argument combining theological continuity, geographical conquest, and ancient legal traditions. Because David is a descendant of Abraham both by the flesh and through their shared faith in God Most High (El Elyon), his claim to the throne of Jerusalem (Salem) operates on three primary levels:

    1. The Legal Right of Global Inheritance (The Abrahamic Title):
    In the ancient world, a covenantal blessing carried immense legal weight, equivalent to a modern deed of inheritance.
  • The Handshake at Salem:
    Centuries prior, Melchizedek formally blessed Abraham in the name of El Elyon (Genesis 14). By accepting this blessing and paying a tithe to Melchizedek, Abraham legally and spiritually connected his lineage to the mysterious king.
  • The Transfer of Custody:
    When Melchizedek's lineage vanished from history, the custodial right to that pure, pre-Flood monotheistic faith did not vanish. It was preserved in the family of Abraham. As Abraham’s direct descendant, David represents the physical and spiritual manifestation of that ancient encounter. David is essentially saying: "The authority that Melchizedek once possessed was legally seeded into my forefather Abraham, and I am the rightful heir to Abraham's estate.
    2. The Law of Geographic and Spiritual Succession:
    In the ancient Near East, kingship was intimately tied to the land and its protective deities. When a new king conquered an ancient, sacred city, he did not just win a military victory; he inherited the spiritual history and royal mantle of that city's founding dynasty.
  • Reclaiming the Throne:
    Jerusalem (Salem) was Melchizedek’s capital. For centuries after Melchizedek, it fell into the hands of the pagan Jebusites. When David conquered Jerusalem (2 Samuel 5), he didn't just build a new palace; David liberated the ancient seat of El Elyon.
  • The Melchizedek Prerogative:
    By physically sitting on the throne of Jerusalem, David could legitimately argue that he was reviving the dormant, interrupted line of Salem's original, righteous kingship. He was stepping into the vacuum left by the disappeared dynasty, positioning himself not as an innovator, but as a restorer of the City of True Light, ancient heritage.
    3. Bypassing the Limitations of the Mosaic Law:
    This argument provides David with a brilliant solution to a major constitutional problem within Israel. Under the Law of Moses, the offices of King and Priest were strictly separated:
  • Kings had to come from the Tribe of Judah (David’s line)
  • Priests had to come from the Tribe of Levi (Aaron's line).
If David tried to act as a priest in the Tabernacle, he would be violating the Mosaic covenant. However, by invoking the "Order of Melchizedek" (as seen in Psalm 110), David invokes a higher, older, and more universal law. His argument is absolute: "My right to lead spiritually does not come from Moses or Levi. It comes from an older covenant—the one shared by Melchizedek and Abraham before the law ever existed. As the King of Jerusalem and the son of Abraham, I inherit the unified role of Priest-King". David’s claim is justified because he acts as the bridge where the bloodline of Abraham, the faith in the Most High, and the physical throne of Jerusalem finally reunite. He uses his lineage to prove his right to Abraham’s blessings, his conquest to prove his right to Melchizedek's city, and their shared faith in El Elyon to shatter the Mesopotamian and Canaanite pantheons, establishing a unified, monotheistic kingdom.

This thesis—often referred to by biblical scholars as the "Jebusite Succession Theory" or the "Zadokite/Melchizedek Legitimation Hypothesis"—is definitively not novel. It has been a highly respected, mainstream academic theory for nearly a century.

The Origins:

The "Myth and Ritual" and German Critical Schools

    The thesis was popularized in the early-to-mid 20th century by prominent German biblical scholars like Hermann Gunkel (the father of Form Criticism) and later expanded upon by British and Scandinavian scholars of the "Myth and Ritual" school (such as Sigmund Mowinckel).   • The Core Academic Argument:
    These scholars noted that in the ancient Near East, kingship was fundamentally priestly. When **David captured the Jebusite city of Jerusalem**, he did not ethnically cleanse it. Instead, he integrated the local population. To unite his newly conquered Canaanite/Jebusite subjects with his loyal Israelite tribes, David (or his court scribes) adopted the pre-existing, ancient local title of the **Priest-King of Salem** (Melchizedek) to legitimize his absolute rule over both groups.

The Smoking Guns:

David's Bizarre Priestly Behavior

    Scholars who champion this thesis point to explicit, strange descriptions in the Books of Samuel where David acts exactly like Melchizedek, completely ignoring the Law of Moses:
  • Sacrificing and Blessing:When David brings the Ark of the Covenant into Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6), he wears a linen ephod (a garment strictly reserved for priests), offers animal sacrifices, and personally blesses the nation. Under the Law of Moses, a king doing this would be struck down or cursed (as King Saul was when he tried to offer a sacrifice). David gets away with it because he is operating under the older Melchizedek rules.
  • David's Sons Were Priests:
    In the official registry of David’s royal administration, 2 Samuel 8:18 explicitly states in the original Hebrew: "And David's sons were priests (Kohanim)". Because his sons were from the Tribe of Judah and not Levi, this verse has deeply troubled traditional commentators for millennia. Academic scholars point to this as clear evidence that the Davidic dynasty claimed a non-Levitical, royal priesthood inherited from the city's ancient history. Thus, the Law of Moses was temporary in nature, serving as a pedagogical guide to direct the people toward the ultimate fulfillment of God’s promise—originally revealed to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. This fulfillment is realized through the cosmic enthronement of the Word of God, who was first given to humanity in Eden. By this divine act, the long-awaited synthesis of the eternal Kingdom and the primordial Priesthood is fully achieved in the Messiah.

The "Zadok" Connection:

Another major sector of biblical scholarship focuses on Zadok, the mysterious high priest who suddenly appears out of nowhere during David's reign to share power with the Levite priest Abiathar.
  • Many critical historians (such as Harvard scholar Frank Moore Cross or H.H. Rowley) have argued that Zadok was actually the original Jebusite high priest of Jerusalem. When David took the city, he kept Zadok on the payroll to maintain local religious continuity. The name Zadok even shares the exact same Hebrew root (Z-D-K, meaning "righteousness") as Melchizedek ("My King is Righteousness") and Adonizedek (the Jebusite king of Jerusalem mentioned in Joshua 10). By installing Zadok, David effectively married the ancient Melchizedek priesthood of Jerusalem into the religion of Israel.
