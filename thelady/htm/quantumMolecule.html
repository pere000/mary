Subatomic and Molecular Quantum Configurations
Patterns, Conformations, and Physical Organization

Main concepts:

  • - Subatomic: particle-level physical configurations.
  • - Molecular: atomic and molecular organization.
  • - Quantum configurations: underlying physical states and relations.
  • - Patterns: organized quantum structures.
  • - Conformations: particular molecular arrangements. Space-Time (S–T) and Electromagnetic–Torsional (E–T).
  • - Physical organization: progression toward macroscopic entities.
Conformations: Particular physically actualized molecular arrangements, characterized by their Space-Time (S–T) and Electromagnetic–Torsional (E–T) determinations. S–T determines their physical configuration and spatial-temporal manifestation, while E–T characterizes their electromagnetic and torsional organization, including the energetic and structural conditions associated with their molecular identity.

This gives you three distinct concepts:
Concept Meaning
E–T configuration Organized range of possible molecular configurations and their associated Markovian states.
S–T conformation Particular physically actualized molecular arrangement, characterized by its spatial and temporal properties, which can be experimentally observed or determined.
E–T determination of a conformation Its physically actualized electromagnetic and torsional organization, whose properties can be experimentally measured or theoretically inferred.

Scientific qualification

This is a proposed ontological formulation from the TACE framework, not a standard scientific definition of a particle, atom, or molecule. In particular, the Markovian description requires a sufficiently specified state space and transition structure. The scientific validity of treating the entire E–T configuration as a Markovian state space would need to be established separately.

1. Particle

Definition

A particle is a determinate quantum-field excitation whose physical identity is constituted by an organized set of possible and actualized quantum states, characterized by its intrinsic properties, interaction relations, and lawful transitions.

Conceptual explanation

A particle can therefore be conceptualized as a quantum-field excitation possessing a determinate organization of possible and actualized states.

  1. Quantum-field constitution: A particle is described physically as an excitation of a quantum field, rather than necessarily as a permanently localized classical object.
  2. Intrinsic properties and interactions: Its identity is characterized by properties such as mass, electric charge, spin, and other applicable quantum numbers, together with its possible interaction relations.
  3. Quantum-state organization: Its physical description encompasses possible quantum states, their superpositions where applicable, and actualized measurement outcomes. These must not be conflated.
  4. Markovian organization: Its evolution can be represented through Markovian states when the state description contains sufficient information for the relevant transition dynamics to depend on the present state.

Particle reality and dynamic organization

A real particle is not adequately represented by a fixed picture, a localized point, or an isolated measurement outcome. Its physical reality must be considered in terms of its quantum-field constitution, its possible states, its actualized configurations, and the lawful interactions and transitions in which it participates.

Geometrical and topological determination

A. Ontological geometry: Establishes the formal relational organization of the particle and its possible quantum states.

B. Physical topology: Expresses its physical actualization through quantum-field configurations, spatial relations, and interactions.

C. Physical properties: Characterize the resulting particle organization, including its mass, charge, spin, and other applicable quantum properties.

2. Atomic structure

Definition

An atomic structure is a determinate quantum-field complex whose physicochemical identity is constituted by the organized relations among its nucleus and electrons, establishing its characteristic nuclear, electronic, energetic, and chemical properties.

Conceptual explanation

An atom can therefore be conceptualized as a quantum-field complex possessing a determinate organization of nuclear and electronic states.

  1. Quantum-field constitution: The atomic system is constituted through quantum fields and their interactions, including the electromagnetic interaction and the interactions governing nuclear organization.
  2. Nuclear organization: The nucleus consists of protons and neutrons, themselves composite quantum systems. Its organization determines the atomic number, isotope, nuclear properties, and contributes to the atom's mass.
  3. Electronic organization: Electrons occupy quantum states associated with the atomic system. Their configurations and interactions with the nucleus determine electronic structure, energy levels, and much of the atom's chemical behavior.
  4. E–T organization: Electromagnetic interactions and the relevant electronic and nuclear configurations participate in determining the atom's formal organization. Torsional configurations become relevant where the atomic system participates in larger molecular or composite structures.
  5. Markovian organization: The atomic system encompasses possible and actualized nuclear and electronic states, together with the transition conditions governing changes between them.

Atomic reality and dynamic organization

An actual atom is not simply a static arrangement of a nucleus surrounded by electrons following classical trajectories. It is a dynamically organized quantum system whose identity is maintained through the organization and lawful evolution of its nuclear and electronic states. Its atomic identity is principally determined by its nuclear charge, specifically its proton number. Changes in neutron number produce different isotopes; changes in electron number produce ions without changing the element's atomic identity.

Geometrical and topological determination

A. Ontological geometry: Establishes the formal relational organization of the atomic system, including the relations among its constituent quantum structures.

B. Physical topology: Expresses the physical actualization of that organization through its spatial and spatiotemporal configuration, nuclear organization, and electronic distribution.

C. Physicochemical properties: Characterize the resulting atomic organization, including its mass, charge state, energy levels, electronic behavior, and chemical reactivity.

3. Molecule

Definition

A molecule is a determinate quantum-field complex whose physicochemical identity is constituted by an organized E–T configuration, establishing its characteristic structural, energetic, and chemical properties.

Conceptual explanation

A molecule can therefore be conceptualized as a quantum-field complex possessing a determinate E–T configuration.

  1. Quantum-field constitution: The molecule is constituted through quantum fields and their interactions, rather than being merely a collection of classical particles.
  2. E–T organization: Electromagnetic interactions and torsional configurations participate in determining its formal organization.
  3. E–T configuration: The organized set of possible space-time conformations and corresponding Markovian states through which a molecule can assume distinct physical configurations, subject to the lawful constraints governing their actualization and transitions.

This entails three complementary aspects:

A. E–T configuration: The organized range of possible molecular configurations and their associated Markovian states.

B. Spatiotemporal conformations: The particular molecular arrangements that can be physically actualized.

C. Markovian states: The presently actualized molecular states, whose lawful succession depends on the present state and its applicable transition structure.

Molecular reality and dynamic organization

A real molecule is not a fixed picture of its structure—a snapshot, an arbitrary cut, or an isolated moment in its dynamics—but a determinate organization of its set of possible and actualized Markovian conformations, including their dynamical trajectories and transition possibilities, whose lawful succession preserves its physicochemical identity.

This introduces two complementary levels:

  1. Markovian conformations: Individual conformational states characterized by their presently actualized organization and applicable transition conditions.
  2. Markovian subsets of conformations: Organized collections of conformational states related through lawful transitions, preserving relevant molecular identity across changes.

The formulation establishes three important distinctions:

  1. Representation versus reality: A snapshot or arbitrary cut captures only a partial determination of the molecule.
  2. Static configuration versus dynamic organization: Molecular identity is considered across multiple possible and actualized conformations.
  3. Change versus continuity: Lawful transitions permit conformational change while preserving physicochemical identity.

Geometrical and topological determination

Its ontological geometry establishes the formal relational structure, while its physical topology expresses the actualization of that organization in the Physical Realm through molecular spatiotemporal configuration, bonding relations, and the resulting physicochemical properties.

A. Ontological geometry: Establishes the formal relational structure.

B. Physical topology: Expresses its physical actualization in space-time.

C. Physicochemical properties: Characterize the resulting physically actualized molecular organization.

Molecular identity and continuity

  • E–T configuration is not a single molecular conformation. It encompasses the organized possibilities through which different conformations and Markovian states can be actualized.
  • Molecular identity is not identical to any one Markovian state. It is preserved through the lawful succession of relevant states.
