You have likely already noticed the various ways the word "space" is used in literature. That is, human consciousness cannot escape the notion of space, it is constantly presented to us; try imagining something without that notion—frankly, I do not believe any human being is capable of doing so. Thus, space is everywhere; we ourselves are made of space, and even our souls, spirits, consciousness and thoughts must have their own spaces.

How can there be distinction between ideas or concepts without a form of space? Independently of temporal succession, if we did not, in some sense, apprehend a space between two ideas, we could not distinguish one from the other. Distinct intentional objects require at least an ontological separation. Every being is what it is by virtue of its boundary or limit—the boundary that constitutes its identity, much like the border between two countries or the boundary of an ecological system. Without such a boundary, no entity could be distinguished from another.

The word intelligence derives ultimately from the Latin intellegentia, from intellegō (inter + legō). In its original sense, the verb denotes the act of discerning or picking out what is significant from among alternatives. The prefix inter ("between," "among") suggests the space of distinctions or relations, while legō, legere means "to pick out," "choose," "select," "gather," and, by extension, "read."

From this perspective, intelligence is intrinsically semantic: it is the capacity to discern relevant differences within a field of possibilities and thereby constitute meaningful unity. Its ontology is therefore revealed through its etymology. To understand is not merely to accumulate information, but to recognize the distinctions and relations that organize experience into coherent meaning.

The principal meanings of "legō, legere" include: (1) to read, (2) to choose, (3) to select, (4) to gather, (5) to collect, and (6) to pick out.
