5. Neuropsychic Inference versus Conscious Perception
Knowledge does not necessarily imply consciousness. An entity may possess enormous quantities of information, perform sophisticated reasoning, learn from experience, predict future events, and adapt its behavior without ever becoming consciously aware of what it knows.

Modern artificial intelligence provides an illuminating example. Advanced AI systems frequently appear to understand a person's emotions, intentions, memories, beliefs, and preferences. In reality, however, they possess no conscious awareness of those mental states. Instead, they construct increasingly accurate predictive models by analyzing vast quantities of linguistic, behavioral, contextual, physiological, and statistical information.

This is what Georgiev D. D. (2023) asserts regarding consciousness in his work "Evolution of Consciousness" (Life [Basel, Switzerland], 14(1), 48).
The essence of consciousness is in the private phenomenological character of conscious experiences. For example, when we look at the blue sky or when we inhale the salt air from the ocean, there is a particular feeling that we experience for each situation, and there is no particular behavior that is expected from us. Consequently, our consciousness is not a kind of behavior or some form of action, but rather a state of existence, which we refer to as a sentient state or a mental state. This mental state changes dynamically in time, creating a flow of feelings that comprise the stream of conscious experience.

TACE, however, defines that "state of existence" as the result of a "mode of being": a mode consisting of the "capacity for self-awareness," observable provided the system has not developed a functional anomaly related to consciousness. On the other hand, although TACE aligns with Maturana's theory of knowledge, there is insufficient evidence to confirm the evolution of cognitive processes toward reflexivity regarding one's own internal experience. It has not been demonstrated that hallucinatory states in animals—such as those induced by hallucinogens—lead to reflection on the self; rather, they lead to the destruction of the autopoiesis of the animal's own world and of the animal itself. Cognition does not consist in asking why stimuli are the way they are, which is an object of consciousness, but rather in reacting in accordance with the neuropsychic system's predictions.
