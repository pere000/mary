TACE Theology: An Introduction to the Logos

The Ontological Foundation for a Dialogue Between the Logos and Creatures

If the If the Interface Theory of Perception (ITP) is treated as a universal condition of all consciousness, it risks making the constraints of the physical realm universal as well. However, this conclusion depends on how broadly the theory is interpreted. Hoffman’s ITP proposes that perceptual experiences function as interfaces shaped by fitness, rather than necessarily revealing reality as it exists independently of perception. It does not, by itself, establish that every possible form of consciousness must be constrained by the physical realm.

The critical issue is the transition from a theory of human perception to a universal ontology of consciousness. If angelic consciousness, for example, can apprehend intelligible content without the perceptual and inferential mediation characteristic of embodied creatures, then treating ITP as a necessary condition for all consciousness would either exclude that mode of being or require an expanded account of what constitutes an interface. The same issue arises with other, still hypothetical, forms of existence endowed with consciousness but lacking the organic organization characteristic of human beings and other terrestrial creatures.

Given that the observable domain of the Interface Theory of Perception (ITP) is constrained by the perceptual conditions it investigates, the theory should not be assumed to establish a universal condition for consciousness. Nevertheless, given the vastness and potentially unbounded diversity of the universe, the existence of other modes of being endowed with consciousness remains plausible. Furthermore, differences in the environments and modes of being of such conscious entities may give rise to distinct perceptual interfaces. Consequently, it is plausible that multiple forms of ITP may exist, each reflecting the particular conditions under which a given mode of consciousness interacts with and apprehends its environment.



From the possibility of a dialogue between the Logos and creatures raises a fundamental theological issue: how beings can posses distinct modes of existence to participate in a reality in which truth, meaning, and intelligibility are shared.

In Christian theology, the Logos is not merely an abstract principle of communication or a universal structure of meaning. The Prologue of John identifies the Logos as being with God and as God, and declares that the Logos became flesh (John 1:1, 14). The Logos, therefore, belongs to the core of Christian existence: the relationship between God and creation, divine intelligibility, and the possibility of freedom for both the Creator and creatures are fundamental theological concerns. Yet this shared horizon of freedom constitutes a profound mystery. For the Creator, it entails the possibility of being challenged—and, in the language of this inquiry, tempted—to become a mere, immoral technocrat: a ruler who reduces creation to an object of technical management, subordinating its meaning and freedom to instrumental control.


Freedom Between the Creator and Creatures: An Ontological Perspective

It could be argued that the degree, scope, or depth of the freedom God shares with His creatures is subject to constraints arising not from any intrinsic superiority of the Creator, but from ontological impossibility. The fundamental principle is that 0 can never be equal to 1, just as a proposition cannot be both true and false at the same time and in the same respect. These principles express the impossibility of collapsing distinct determinations into contradictory identities.

The limits of shared freedom must therefore be understood in relation to the wisdom and power that God's eternity rationally entails. Divine omnipotence does not require the realization of logical or ontological contradictions; rather, it operates without making the impossible possible by abolishing the principles that distinguish truth from falsehood and being from non-being. This argument seeks to establish the conditions of the relationship between the Creator and creatures through rational inquiry, without requiring the prior acceptance of faith.

For example, angels require neither faith nor discursive reasoning to enter into dialogue with any conscious being or to interact with anything. They do not reason discursively to discover the truth; rather, the truth manifests itself directly in response to the question, without delay or inferential mediation. By virtue of their mode of being, the content of cognitive forms presents itself to them immediately. This distinctive mode of apprehension renders their act of freedom unique and ensures that its merit is fully and definitively established, even though it may not have been consummated within physical history.

The situation, however, is fundamentally different for physical creatures, whose freedom unfolds under conditions of mediation, temporal succession, and incomplete apprehension. Their decisions take place over the course of physical history, where knowledge is acquired progressively and the significance of available possibilities is not necessarily grasped in its entirety.

The difference between angelic and physical freedom, therefore, concerns not simply the degree of power available to each creature, but the conditions under which each exercises freedom. Distinct modes of being entail distinct modes of apprehension and, consequently, distinct conditions for the exercise and completion of free acts. Thus, the central question is how God can share freedom with creatures possessing different modes of being while preserving the ontological distinctions that make their relationship possible. The answer cannot consist in the abolition of those distinctions, since doing so would entail contradiction. Rather, the relationship must be understood through the wisdom and power that God's eternity rationally entails, together with the ontological conditions under which created freedom is actualized.

In this sense, the relationship between divine and creaturely freedom constitutes a profound mystery, which is why the Creator continually serves all creatures in the exercise of their freedom for the well-being of the whole of Creation. It raises the question of how freedom can be genuinely shared without collapsing the distinction between Creator and creature, and how the conditions of this sharing differ according to the modes of being and apprehension proper to each creature.



TACE Theology begins its inquiry at the intersection of these questions. If creatures possess distinct modes of being, and if their apprehension of reality is conditioned by those modes, how can divine truth be communicated to them? What makes creatures capable of receiving, recognizing, and responding to the Logos? How should the relationship between created intelligibility and divine revelation be understood?

The onto-epistemological interface of consciousness provides a conceptual starting point for investigating these questions. It helps distinguish the existence of a creature from the way reality becomes intelligible to that creature. It also opens a path toward examining whether a shared world of intelligibility can provide a basis for dialogue without reducing the Creator to creation or treating divine revelation as merely another perceptual interface.

**TACE Theology proposes to investigate the relationship between the Logos and creatures through the distinctions among being, consciousness, intelligibility, and revelation.** Its purpose is to explore how creatures with different modes of being may stand in relation to divine truth while preserving the ontological distinction between the Necessary Being and created beings.

This is an introduction to the inquiry, not its conclusion. The central task is to clarify how the metaphysical conditions of creaturely existence and intelligibility relate to the Christian understanding of the Logos, without confusing a philosophical account of communication with the mystery of divine self-revelation.

*The dialogue between the Logos and creatures begins, therefore, not with the assumption that all beings apprehend reality in the same way, but with the question of how divine truth can address creatures according to their own modes of being.*
