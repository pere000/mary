The Hard Problem of Consciousness (Chalmers 1995) is as hard as ever, despite the best efforts of scientific <br>
endeavor; there is, as yet, <b>no satisfactory explanation for a sense of self</b> or a satisfactory explanation <br>
for why we are conscious at all. The point at which many scientists, philosophers, and lay psychonauts diverge is <br>
on the question of whether these worlds are real, autonomous realities, or simply products of a hallucinatory <br>
state of mind. However, this distinction between reality and hallucination begins to blur when we appreciate that <br>
‘consensus reality’, the world we all live in, is constructed and represented by the brain and that the experience <br>
of a ‘world out there’ is an illusion, though not a hallucination. The suggestion here is not to trivialize <br>
consensus reality—the brain’s ability to construct a world of such beauty and complexity is remarkable; a world <br>
that enables us to survive and flourish in whatever is ‘out there’.<br>

Let us focus on the term used for objective reality: the external world. Consequently, objective reality is not <br>
the internal world. If consciousness is understood as an internal space, it follows that the external world must <br>
somehow be represented within the interior of the human body. However, the external world is far too vast and <br>
substantial to be physically contained within the human body, in a space referred to as consciousness. <br>
Consequently, it is evident that radical changes must therefore occur in the way external reality can be <br>
represented, so that it may be integrated into the internal space of the human body that we call consciousness.<br>

We know that the internal space of the human body is composed of substances of the same general nature as those <br>
found in the external world, and that the latter cannot, in any way, be compressed to occupy the space that the <br>
human being occupies. Accordingly, as Heraclitus observed, things are always flowing and changing, both out there <br>
and in here. We can therefore assume that one way for us to become aware of what is happening out there is to <br>
notice changes occurring inside us concomitantly with changes occurring outside us. In other words, we do not need <br>
any substance from out there to be physically located in here; it is sufficient for something inside us to change <br>
concomitantly with what is happening outside us.<br>

In short, we do not need external substances themselves to be reproduced inside us; we need to apprehend the <br>
changes occurring in them, whether these involve their accidents, their internal physical configurations, <br>
quantum-state transformations, or other forms of physical transformation. Our own substances undergo corresponding <br>
changes through which we become capable of apprehending what is happening beyond our boundaries. It means that for <br>
Hoffman’s Interface Theory of Perception, the changes involved in perception are limited to those that optimize <br>
evolutionary fitness and survival. By contrast, within the Hylomorphic Interface Hypothesis of Consciousness, such <br>
changes are conditioned by the organism’s mode of being and by the existence of the noumenon, rather than being <br>
determined by evolutionary utility.<br>

In both cases, consciousness does not require mental images of the spatial and temporal properties of external <br>
substances, or what might be called "icons of reality"; it apprehends the "world out there" through <br>
representational records of structural and dynamic changes, or through abstract mathematical isomorphisms, which <br>
may be understood as "guts of reality". This could offer a possible explanation for the apparently alien imagery <br>
encountered during DMT experiences, in which perceptual representations may depart radically from the familiar <br>
spatial and temporal organization of ordinary experience, perhaps revealing alternative ways in which underlying <br>
structural and relational information can be represented, including the user's own intelligence dynamics <br>
personifying the entities.<br>
