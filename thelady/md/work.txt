No, a quantum subatomic particle does not occupy a sharp, rigidly fenced physical space domain into the quantum field within which its effects are strictly contained.
Instead, the "space domain" of a particle in Quantum Field Theory (QFT) is defined by a mathematical concept called localization, which dictates that its ability to exert an effect drops off smoothly rather than hitting a hard boundary.
Here is exactly how a particle interacts with space and its field to exert an effect:

1. The Mathematical Domain (The Wave Packet)

In QFT, a particle is a localized excitation (a ripple) in a quantum field. To confine a particle to a specific region, physicists construct a wave packet.
• The particle has a high probability of being detected within the core "domain" of this packet.
• However, the mathematical tail of this wave packet technically extends infinitely outward. It never drops to absolute zero at a sharp boundary line.

2. The Limits of Space: Reeh-Schlieder Theorem

In quantum field theory, a profound mathematical principle known as the Reeh-Schlieder Theorem proves that it is fundamentally impossible to isolate a quantum state completely within a strictly bounded region of space.
Because vacuum fluctuations are entangled across all space domains, a particle in one region technically holds a non-zero (though astronomically small) quantum relationship with fields everywhere else in the universe.

3. The Effect Domain (The Interaction Range)

If we define the "space domain" by where a particle can exert a force or effect, that domain is dictated by the mass of the field's force-carrier:

Force Carrier	Mass	Space Domain / Range of Effect
Photon (Electromagnetism)	Massless	Infinite. The field dims with distance (\(1/r^2\)) but has no structural edge.
Gluon (Strong Force)	Massless	Extremely Restricted (~\(10^{-15}\) meters). Even though gluons are massless, they attract each other so violently that they bind quarks tightly into a tiny, defined composite spatial domain (like a proton).
W/Z Bosons (Weak Force)	Massive	Ultra-short (~\(10^{-18}\) meters). Because the carriers are heavy, the effect drops off exponentially fast (\(e^{-mr}/r\)), creating a highly localized domain of effect.


Visualizing Localization

To see how a particle exerts its effect across space, we look at its spatial distribution. Rather than a hard-walled box, the domain behaves like a bell curve (Gaussian distribution). The particle's presence and immediate effects are concentrated at the center, but the boundaries fade gradually into the background quantum vacuum.

4. What Happens if You Force a Particle Into a Tight Domain?

If you try to compress a subatomic particle into a highly specific, microscopic space domain, the Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle takes over. By restricting its spatial domain (\(\Delta x\)), you inject massive amounts of momentum uncertainty (\(\Delta p\)).
If the domain is forced to become smaller than the particle's Compton wavelength, the energy concentrated in that space becomes so volatile that it triggers the field to create new particle-antiparticle pairs. Instead of isolating one particle, the field creates a cloud of many.
